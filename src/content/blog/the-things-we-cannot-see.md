<blockquote>
  We may be in the Universe as dogs and cats are in our libraries, seeing the books and hearing the conversation, but having no inkling of the meaning of it all.
  <br />
  <em>- William James</em>
</blockquote>

What if some of our deepest assumptions are wrong?

Not the ones about technology or economics, but more fundamental ones, like the belief that what we perceive is a reasonable approximation of reality.

Most of us move through the world as though reality and perception are roughly the same thing. We know our senses have limits, of course. We cannot see UV-light or detect magnetic fields. But these limitations often feel technical rather than profound. We assume that while we may miss some details, we are at least perceiving the important parts.

I’m not so sure.

Because perception is not passive. Not just a camera pointed at the world.

<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Invisible_Gorilla" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Attention selects. Expectations filter</a>. Prior experience shapes interpretation.

We do not simply see the world as it is. We construct our version of it. And the models we apply to reality determine not only what we understand, but what we can see.

Cognitive psychologists sometimes <a href="https://youtu.be/lyu7v7nWzfo?si=BLZDHmh638oRxc_2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">describe perception as a kind of ”controlled hallucination”</a>. The brain generates predictions about the world and updates them using incoming sensory information, in perpetual feed forward calibration. What we experience is not raw reality, but an active negotiation between expectation and sensation.

In other words: we don’t just believe what we see. To a surprising degree, we see what we *believe*.

This becomes particularly interesting when we encounter something that does not fit our models.

Just for a moment, imagine that something genuinely strange existed alongside us. Not necessarily extraterrestrial intelligence. Just something completely and fundamentally outside our expectations.

Would we recognize it? Or would we interpret it through familiar categories? Dismiss it as noise? Or fail to notice it altogether?

All this becomes easier to relate to if we turn our attention away from hypothetical visitors, and toward the living world around us.

A century ago, the biologist Jakob von Uexküll introduced the concept of Umwelt, the idea that every organism inhabits its own perceptual world: A tick experiences reality through heat and the scent of butyric acid. A bat sees through echoes. A migratory bird senses magnetic fields. A bee perceives ultraviolet patterns completely invisible to us. Each inhabits a different slice of the same world.

<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Immense_World" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">As Ed Yong writes</a>: ”Earth teems with sights and textures, sounds and vibrations, smells and tastes, electric and magnetic fields. But every animal can only tap into a fraction of reality’s fullness.”

What we call reality is partly the world itself, and partly this particular sliver which our senses allow us to access.

Thomas Nagel famously asked <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_It_Like_to_Be_a_Bat%3F" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">what it is like to be a bat</a>.

Just imagine inhabiting a world that comes alive only when you call out to it! A world revealed not by light entering the eye, but by sound returning from the darkness. The bat's reality is not simply a human reality with different inputs. It is structured around a fundamentally different relationship between perception and the world.

But perhaps the more unsettling question is what it is like to be something so different from us that we cannot even begin to imagine its experience.

Consider the <a href="https://www.science.org/content/article/humongous-fungus-almost-big-mall-america" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Armillaria fungi of Michigan</a>. One colony is estimated to be thousands of years old and spread across several square kilometres. It may be among the largest and oldest living organisms on Earth.

What counts as an event for such a being? What counts as a neighbour? What counts as a lifetime? These categories themselves may no longer apply.

We share this planet with forms of life whose Umwelt barely overlap with our own. Not because they are hidden, but because they operate at scales of space, time and perception that differ radically from ours.

And if this is true for organisms we already know, what confidence should we have that our own perceptual scales are somehow the most relevant ones?

This thought returns to me whenever discussions about extraterrestrial life arise. Because when we search for intelligence elsewhere in the universe, we tend to look within a surprisingly narrow frame. We search for signals we know how to detect, produced by entities operating on timescales we can comprehend and expressing the type of intelligence that we can recognize.

The assumption lurking beneath much of this search is that our particular slice of reality is somehow the more central one.

But what if it isn’t?

Just toying with the idea, what if UFO sightings (assuming some of them represent something genuinely unexplained) are to us what a laser pointer is to a cat?

A brief flicker at the edge of perception. Something that captures attention without revealing much about the thing producing it.

The point isn’t whether UFOs are real, but that our inability to understand something may reveal as much about the limits of our perception as it does about the phenomenon itself.

Standing in a forest, I sometimes find this thought strangely humbling.

Our perceptual world feels complete because it is all we have ever known. We mistake its boundaries for the boundaries of reality itself.

Yet every organism inhabits a different world. Every species perceives a different fraction of what is there. And even within our own species, attention continuously determines what becomes visible and what remains hidden.

And perhaps what we need is not to gather more information. But to cultivate the ability to notice the assumptions through which we perceive in the first place.

To <a href="https://longreads.com/2026/06/02/jenny-odell-deep-listening-soft-eyes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">soften our gaze</a>.

To become a little less certain that the world we experience is the whole world.

And a little more humbled by the countless ways of being, sensing and inhabiting reality that exist beyond our own.
